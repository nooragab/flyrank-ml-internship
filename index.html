Which Pages to Review First

A validated model for prioritizing content refresh decisions, built on a 100,000-page slice of a 79-million-row search warehouse.

Nour Ragab · Refresh / Content Opportunity Scoring lane · March–April 2026 data

Abstract

Content teams cannot review every page they own, so this work asks which pages to look at first, using a page's observable March 2026 search signals to predict whether its daily impressions fall in April. A transparent baseline rule is compared against logistic regression and random forest classifiers under client-grouped validation. A leakage audit found that two staleness features reflected the data release date rather than March, so they were removed and every result was re-run. In 5-fold client-grouped cross-validation, overall discrimination is weak for all three methods (AUC about 0.60), but the random forest scored higher than the baseline at the top of the ranking in all five folds (Precision@500 of 0.80 versus 0.70, with a base rate of 0.64). On a single held-out split, a top-5% review queue from the random forest had a 68% decline rate versus 54% across all pages. These results are directional, observational decision support for a human-reviewed queue, not a causal or automated system.

Introduction

A content team with dozens or hundreds of pages and limited editorial hours cannot review everything with equal attention. Some pages are worth revisiting — refreshed facts, tightened titles, expanded thin sections — before they lose the visibility they already have. The problem this work addresses is narrow and practical: given a page's recent, observable search performance, which pages deserve review first?

The decision this supports belongs to a content editor or SEO lead operating under limited weekly review capacity. A wrong recommendation has a real but bounded cost — time spent reviewing a page that did not need it, or a genuinely declining page left unreviewed a week longer. This is a decision-support problem, not a fully automated one: the model orders a queue, and a person decides what to do with each entry.

Data

This study uses the FlyRank/internship-warehouse release on Hugging Face (build flyrank_pseudonymized_warehouse_release_v20260703), a pseudonymized snapshot of real search performance data. The full release holds 78.8 million daily fact rows (fact_content_daily_performance) and 519,606 content items (dim_content), across 104 clients.

Tables used

  • dim_content.parquet — content metadata (word count; creation and last-update dates, which turned out to be unusable as March features, see Methodology).
  • fact_content_daily_performance/month=2026-03/ and month=2026-04/ — daily Google Search Console facts, filtered to rows with confirmed GSC data availability.

Date windows

March 2026 serves as the feature window; April 2026 serves as the label window. The label is never computed from data the model could see at prediction time.

Scope and exclusions

The working slice is restricted to pages with at least 100 March impressions that also have GSC data in April, yielding 100,893 page-rows across 43 clients. GA4 engagement signals were excluded to keep the feature set small and auditable; this is a scoping choice for this iteration, not a data-availability limit. No FlyRank product decision flags (health score, priority score, action type) exist in this release, so none required stripping. Every identifier used — client and content IDs — is a pseudonymized hash; no raw client names, domains, URLs, or search queries appear anywhere in this work.

Methodology

Baseline

A transparent, hand-written rule serves as the bar to beat: score = impressions × ctr_gap, where ctr_gap is how far a page's click-through rate sits below its position tier's average. High-traffic pages with a large CTR shortfall rank first.

Label

is_declining_next_month = 1 if a page's April average daily impressions (April total ÷ 30) are below its March average daily impressions (March total ÷ 31), else 0. Comparing daily averages avoids the built-in bias from March having one more day than April.

Features

Four signals, giving six numeric model inputs plus the position tier: monthly impressions, average search position, click-through rate (ctr_month, tier_avg_ctr, ctr_gap), and word count.

Models

Logistic regression (with feature standardization) and random forest classifiers were trained on the same features as the baseline.

Leakage audit and removed features

Every feature was checked against three questions: is it computed after the decision point, does its measurement window overlap the label window, and is it a rebuilt product decision flag? The first version of this analysis passed all eight of its features. A later diagnostic showed that this was wrong for two of them: content_updated_date in the release reflects the release time (July 2026), not 31 March, so 82% of pages had an update date after the decision point. days_since_last_update and is_stale were therefore built partly from information that did not exist at prediction time. The stale flag was also almost constant (99.9% of pages scored zero), which meant the original baseline rule ranked nearly every page at zero. Both features were removed, the baseline was redefined, and all results below were re-run. word_count is also a release-time snapshot that could not be checked against March; it is kept and listed as a limitation.

Validation design

Pages are split by GroupShuffleSplit grouped by client, and robustness is checked with 5-fold GroupKFold cross-validation on the same grouping, so no client's pages appear in both training and test. Cross-validation is the primary result; the single split is directional only. A leakage demonstration (random forest, three seeds) compared a random page-level split with a client-grouped split: Precision@500 was about 0.83 versus 0.80, a small gap. In the earlier version that still contained the release-time feature the gap was larger (about 0.91 versus 0.76), which is consistent with that feature acting as a client-specific signal; this explanation was not tested directly.

On reproducibility: an earlier version of this analysis produced precision values that changed between reruns. Logistic regression was failing to converge on unscaled features. After standardizing the features and raising the iteration limit, the model converges in 12 iterations (limit 2,000) and the warning disappeared. Because the old setup was not re-run for comparison, unscaled features are the most likely cause rather than a confirmed one. With the data frozen to a fixed file, seeds fixed, and ties broken deterministically, two full reruns produced the same data hash and identical cross-validation, leakage, and queue tables. The frozen input file is kept out of the public repository, so a reader must rebuild it from the Hugging Face release and may see small differences if the upstream data changes.

Limitations

  • Observational, not causal. This work shows association between March signals and a later decline, not that refreshing a flagged page causes recovery — that would require a controlled experiment.
  • Single month pair. Trained and evaluated on one March→April transition. Other months and strong seasonal shifts are untested.
  • 43 clients. The model has not been validated on client types outside this population. Pages from one client are correlated, so uncertainty on queue-level figures is wider than a simple sample-size margin suggests.
  • Weak overall discrimination. AUC is about 0.60 for the baseline, logistic regression, and random forest alike. The random forest's advantage is confined to the top of the ranking and rests on five folds.
  • Small-sample metrics. Precision@50 is computed over very few pages, with fold-to-fold standard deviations up to about 0.13. The single split depends on which clients land in the test set, so cross-validation is the primary result and the queue comparison is illustrative.
  • Release-time metadata. days_since_last_update and is_stale were removed because the update date reflects the July 2026 release. word_count is also a release-time snapshot that could not be checked against March; it is kept but may carry information from after the decision point.
  • Survivorship in the label. Pages with no GSC data in April are dropped by the join, so the label only covers pages observed in both months. Pages that disappeared entirely are not counted as declines, and the number dropped was not measured here.
  • Tier averages use all pages. tier_avg_ctr is computed over the full dataset, including held-out clients. With four tier values over about 100,000 pages the effect is likely small, but it was not tested.
  • Leakage explanation not tested. The random-versus-grouped gap shrank after removing days_since_last_update, which is consistent with that feature acting as a client-specific signal, but this was not tested directly.
  • Reason codes are rule-based. They are assigned by a simple CTR and position rule on top of the model ranking, not learned by the model.

All claims in this paper use observed, measured, directional, or decision-support language. Nothing here claims to reverse-engineer a Google ranking factor, nor to prove that any specific action causes a specific outcome.

Human review is required before acting

Before acting on any recommendation, a reviewer should confirm the page is still live and business-relevant, check whether the flagged issue still holds on the live page, and rule out an explanation the model cannot see — a seasonal campaign ending, a sibling page absorbing demand, or a site migration.

What should never be automated: auto-publishing content changes from the model's score alone; auto-deleting or de-indexing pages; treating the model's probability as a guaranteed forecast; or applying this queue to clients outside its training population without re-validation.

Monitoring and retrain triggers

The queue should be treated as stale, and the pipeline re-run, if: the queue's decline rate on a fresh month's held-out data falls toward the overall decline rate; the feature distribution shifts materially from the March training snapshot; a structurally new client segment appears; or more than roughly three months pass since the last retrain.
